01 — The Concept

Truncate a corner, and the patch stops being symmetric.

A square patch antenna radiates a single linear polarization by default. Cut two opposite corners diagonally, and you excite two orthogonal modes 90° apart — the recipe for circular polarization. Here, those cuts aren't permanent: they're gaps bridged by PIN diodes.

Patch
49.4 × 47.7 mm square, copper on Rogers RT/duroid 5880 (εr = 2.2, h = 1.575 mm), resonant near 2.4 GHz.
Slots
Diagonal cuts near two opposite corners, each stopping short of the patch interior to leave a small connection gap.
Diodes
A PIN diode bridges each gap. ON ≈ 2 Ω + 0.3 nH. OFF ≈ 0.15 pF. Modelled as lumped RLC elements in CST.
Feed
Coaxial probe positioned along the patch diagonal, 50 Ω, offset from center for correct mode excitation.
02 — Switching Sequence

Three states, one antenna, zero moving parts.

01

Both diodes ON

The patch stays fully symmetric. A single dominant mode radiates — linear polarization, same as a conventional patch.

02

One diode OFF

The corresponding corner becomes an open truncation. Two orthogonal modes of near-equal amplitude appear, 90° apart in phase.

03

Circular polarization

Which corner is open sets the rotation sense — RHCP or LHCP — verified by axial ratio below 3 dB at boresight.

03 — System Architecture

The FPGA never touches RF — it just decides.

Digital control and RF signal are on entirely separate paths. An HDL state machine sets the diode bias pattern; a driver and bias network translate that into the DC conditions each diode needs, without loading the RF path.

04 — Field Notes

Terms worth knowing before you read the plots.

METRIC

Axial Ratio

Ratio of the major to minor axis of the polarization ellipse. ≤ 3 dB at boresight is the usual bar for "good enough" circular polarization.

CONCEPT

LP vs. CP

Linear polarization confines the E-field to one plane. Circular polarization rotates it — clockwise (RHCP) or counter-clockwise (LHCP) — as the wave travels.

COMPONENT

PIN Diode Switching

Forward-biased, a PIN diode looks like a small resistor; reverse-biased, a small capacitor. That's enough to make or break an RF current path.
